# Body

## The premise

[2:09]
> "Objections are triggered reactions based on uncertainty in your prospect's mind. Who controls whether the prospect has certainty or uncertainty? **YOU** do. The salesperson — not the prospect."

Triggers:
- Your tonality
- Your body language (even if they can't see you — affects tone)
- Facial expressions (affect tone)
- The words and questions you choose
- The frames/beliefs prospects have about salespeople

## Preventing the spouse objection (B2C)

### Step 1 — Surface spouse opinion EARLY

[4:46] When prospect mentions a problem/consequence:

> "How does your spouse feel about [REPEAT THE PROBLEM AND CONSEQUENCE]?"

**Examples:**
- "How does your spouse feel about you guys not even having a policy to protect them when you pass away?"
- "How does your spouse feel about you guys paying all those rate hikes with Edison?"

### Why repeat back the SPECIFIC consequence (not vague)

[5:47]
> "Don't say 'how does your spouse feel about this problem?' — too vague. They don't feel it. Plug in the SPECIFIC problem + consequence."

### Examples by industry

**Small business owner (struggling to pay themselves):**
> "How does your spouse feel about you not being able to pay yourself an entire year?"
> "Oh man, she hates it."

**Network marketing / business opportunity:**
> "How does your spouse feel about you having to work 60–70 hours a week?"

**With kids in the picture:**
> "Does she want you to be forced to work 60 hours a week and miss all your son's games?"

**Critical word — "forced":**
> "Nobody wants to be forced or made to do anything. I'm seeding that into the prospect's brain."

### Step 2 — Flip to positive if they don't engage

[7:51]
> "Well, does he want you to be able to work from home so you have time to go to your kids' ball games — or what does he want you to do?"

→ Prospect: "No, he definitely wants me to do that."

> "Now it's hard for them to come back and say 'I need to talk to my spouse' — because they already said the spouse wants the outcome."

## NEPQ Identity Frame for preventing objections

[12:00] After the spouse-feeling questions:

> "I mean, good for you and your spouse looking at this now. I mean, your son is lucky to have parents like you who care so much to spend time with him. I talk to a lot of people out there — and you'd be surprised, a lot of parents don't really care about spending time with their kids. You know what I mean?"

**Mechanism:** Negative identity ("a lot of parents who don't care") = the identity they DON'T want to be. They identify away → commit to being the "good parent" identity.

## Preventing "I want to think it over / more research"

### The truth about "think it over"

[15:41]
> "I need to think it over is NOT a real objection. When you tell a salesperson you want to think it over — do you go home and write out pros and cons for 3 weeks? No. You just said that to get rid of them. They have some concern they don't want to tell you."

### Step 1 — Probing question that emphasizes urgency

[17:13]
> "Can I ask why this is so important to you *(verbal pause)* now though?"

Pause before "now" → brain focuses on **NOW** → builds urgency.

### Step 2 — Negative identity frame after a consequence question

[18:46]
> "I mean, you certainly sound motivated. I mean for you — why look at doing this now though? Like why not push it down the road and keep paying the rate hikes like a lot of homeowners do who don't know anything about solar?"

[20:51]
> "Because they're at their house or virtually — it's hard for them to want to identify with 'this person who keeps paying the rate hikes because they don't know anything about solar.' They move away from that identity."

### Industry-tailored examples

**Solar (homeowner):**
> "…like a lot of homeowners do who don't know anything about solar"

**Marketing agency (small business):**
> "…like a lot of companies do who never actually scale the business or eventually go out of business"

**Marketing agency (mid-size company):**
> "…like a lot of companies do who never end up being able to scale"

**Marketing consultant → billion-dollar company:**
> "…like a lot of companies do and just hope and pray your internal marketing team someday figures it all out"

> "I have to tie that in to what actually makes sense for the prospect and what they actually told me."

## Preventing "Tell me the price / cost"

### Why this objection happens

[24:29]
> "Your tonality sounds too monotone (telemarketer), too timid (nervous), or too excited. There's a middle ground — the expert, the trusted authority. The expert isn't attached to whether you buy. The expert is detached. **The prospect has the problems, not you.**"

[25:31]
> "Or because your questions sound scripted, robotic — like an interrogation. 'Okay cool, gotcha gotcha, John let me ask you' — sounds weird. Bridge with verbal cues — 'oh really, but what happened then?'"

### The reframe

[27:04]
> "Hey Jeremy, I'm out of time, no time for the questions. Can you just tell me the cost and I'll tell you if I'm interested?"

> "Oh yeah, we'll go through that for sure. I mean, it's really going to depend on like your blank, your blank, and your blank. And once I understand all those different details, I can go through all the different options we have for our clients. Would that help you?"

### Industry example — Life insurance

[28:36]
> "Oh yeah, we'll go through that for sure. I mean, it's really going to depend on like your age, your health conditions, your expenses — especially with like the mortgage payment you have to do every month. And once I understand all those different details, I can go through all the policy options we have for clients. Would that help you more if I did that for you?"

### The mechanic analogy

[29:07]
> "It would be like you pull up to a mechanic, throw in the key, and say 'hey, can you fix my car? By the way, how much is it going to cost?' What would the mechanic say? 'Well, I don't know — I don't know what's wrong with the car yet.'"

> "If you don't know what's wrong with their situation — how can you know the price?"

### "Would that help you if I did that for you?"

[30:09]
> "Would that help you if I did that for you?"

> "Why am I asking that? Who has the problems — the prospect or you? The prospect. So why are you qualifying TO them? I'm getting them to qualify TO ME. 'Would that help you if I did that for you?' — see, I'm doing them a favor by solving their problems. Not the other way around."

## Key takeaways / CZ kontext

- **Spouse-feeling question** for CZ small-business owners: *"Jak na to kouká manželka, že ti ten leden je furt prázdnej a nemůžeš vzít celou rodinu na dovolenou?"*
- **"Forced"** in CZ: *"nutí tě"* / *"musíš"* — emotionally loaded. *"Chceš se s tím dál nutit do nepříjemných týdnů?"*
- **"Why is THIS so important to you NOW though?"** with pause: *"Můžu se zeptat, proč je tohle pro tebe *(pauza)* teď tak důležitý?"*
- **Negative identity frame** in CZ for a close, e.g. an e-shop: *"Proč to dělat teď a ne to odkládat jako spousta majitelů e-shopů, co o tom, odkud jim chodí objednávky, vlastně nikdy nezačnou přemýšlet, a pak se diví, proč jim tržby stojí?"*
- **The mechanic analogy** = perfect CZ explainer for "kolik to stojí?": *"To je jako kdybys přijel k mechanikovi, hodil mu klíče a zeptal se: kolik to bude? On ti odpoví: já nevím, ještě nevím, co je s tím autem."*
- **"Would that help you if I did that for you?"** in CZ: *"Pomohlo by ti, kdybych ti to takhle připravila?"* — single highest-leverage qualifying flip.
