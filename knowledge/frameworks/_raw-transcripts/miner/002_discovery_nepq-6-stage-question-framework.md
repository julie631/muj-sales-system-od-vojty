# Body

[0:01] In the last 2 years close to 200,000 people have bought the NEPQ Black Book. I'm going to give you the top questions from this book that you can use for what you sell — phone, in-person, B2B, B2C, B2G.

## Stage 1 — Connection Questions

[0:34] Connection questions do three things:
1. **Disarm the prospect** — get their guard down so they stop giving you "vague, generalized, surface-level answers."
2. **Get them out of price/cost-based thinking** and into **results-based thinking** (because that's the first question every prospect has: "How much is this going to cost?")
3. Begin gathering signal about what drives them.

**Generic connection question** (after pleasantries, e.g. SDR booked the meeting):

> "So when you talked to my associate Jamie, what was it that you guys went over that caused you to feel like, *hey, maybe I need to look into this a bit further?*"

When the prospect tells you why — they're also telling **themselves**. They're moving from price → results.

**Industry-specific examples:**

*Auto (responded to ad):*
> "Yeah, we just got that car in a trade-in that you're looking at. Well, I guess — what aspects of the car caused you to maybe want to come in and test drive it?"

*SaaS (associations):*
> "Looks like you booked with us to look at maybe getting some outside help with your awards program to really automate the process. When you were talking with my associate Dave, what was it you guys went over that caused you to feel like, hey, maybe I need to look into this further?"

## Stage 2 — Situation Questions

[6:46] You're finding out the **facts** of what's going on. But more importantly — **the prospect needs to understand their own real situation**. Most prospects don't fully understand it until your questions force them to articulate it. If they don't understand where they're at, you can't build the gap to where they want to be.

*Marketing agency:*
> "So walk me through — what are you guys doing to generate new leads and clients now? Just so I understand." (curious tone)

*Relationship therapist:*
> "So I was reading your application and you had mentioned that you guys are having some issues around some resentment and communication with your husband. Can you tell me a little bit about what's going on?" (concerned tone)

## Stage 3 — Problem Awareness Questions

[9:20] Why "problem*s*" — plural? Because if you only find one problem, the gap is small. Find 3–5 problems the prospect didn't realize they had → gap becomes massive → they can't solve it on their own, can't go elsewhere, perceive your value as far higher than competitors.

**Two biggest emotional drivers that cause humans to change:**
1. **Pain** (current state or past history)
2. **Fear of future pain** (life insurance, cybersecurity, etc.)

**Anchoring trick — the 100% qualifier:**

*Financial services:*
> "So the retirement strategy you have right now — do you 100% love the returns you've been getting?" *(verbal pacing — slow it down)*

Why 100%? Because nobody is 100% satisfied with anything. "Think about everything you've ever bought. Do you love it 100%? Unlikely." Forces honest answer.

**Reframe instead of "What don't you like?"**

Bad questions (predictable — every prospect sees them coming):
- "What don't you like about your current vendor?"
- "What would you change?"
- "What are two issues you're having?"

NEPQ pattern interrupt:

*Recruiting agency:*
> "So help me understand — you've been posting on these job boards the last four years like Indeed. I mean, what's caused you to feel like Indeed might not be getting you the best people?"

*Real estate:*
> "The home you have is a very beautiful home. I mean, what's caused you to feel like you might want to sell it?"

When they answer, they tell you AND themselves why they want change. **Internal persuasion** beats external persuasion every time.

## Stage 4 — Solution Awareness Questions (Future Pacing)

[14:28] Even if you've built a huge gap, if the prospect can't visualize what life looks/feels like once the problems are gone — there's no sale. Solution awareness = **future pacing**.

*Fitness:*
> "So let's say that we come in, we get you involved in XYZ plan, we help you lose the 105 lbs like we do with our other clients. What would you be able to do that maybe you can't do right now, or maybe you can't do your best at?" (concern tone, empathy)

When they answer ("I'd play basketball with my sons"), probe deeper:
> "Being able to play ball with your sons again — what would that do for you?"

*Health insurance:*
> "So let's suppose we're able to get you a policy that has a nationwide network so you can see any doctor you want and know that when something big happens you're completely covered financially. Knowing that was there — what would that do for you guys?"

## Stage 5 — Consequence Questions

[18:34] What happens if they don't change? Pulled from Brian Tracy. Ask at ~75% of conversation, not early.

*Dental implants:*
> "So what happens if you don't do anything about this? *(slight challenge)* You keep losing the bone density in your jaw, and now you can't even get implants. *(soften to concern)*"

Notice the **tone shift** — start slightly challenging to raise emotion, then drop to concerned/empathetic. "I'm concerned for you if you don't do this."

*Employee benefits (tied to a specific pain they mentioned):*
> "On the flip side — what happens if you just keep the same plan and your top executives keep going over to XYZ company? What's going to happen to the business at that point?"

*Home improvement (pool, tied to grandkids at YMCA):*
> "So what happens if you don't put the pool in? Your grandkids keep driving all the way to the YMCA 30 miles away and you miss out on those summer memories with them."

**Critical rule:** Tie the consequence to what THEY said. Don't make it up. That's how they feel understood.

> "The best question asked at the wrong time can actually be the worst question. You have to know the when, the why, and the how."

## Stage 6 — Commitment Questions (NOT "closing")

[22:12] Miner refuses the word "closing":
> "Does anybody want to be closed? No. But do you want to buy? Yes. Do you want a problem solved? Yes. Do you want emotional needs solved? Yes."

**Micro-commitments** (next-appointment, next-step) lead to the final commitment.

**Generic commitment question** (any industry):
> "Do you feel like this could be the answer for you?"
> — "Yeah, I do."
> "Well, hold on — why do you feel like it is?"

If they say "Yeah, I do, **but**…" → you've **triggered the objection on purpose** instead of letting it ambush you after the call.

> "I want to know the objection right there. I don't want to just hope and pray they don't have one. I want to trigger an objection at the end so I know what it is — not throw dust over it and hope they don't cancel."

*Real estate (seller):*
> "Do you feel like the marketing plan we put together — do you feel like that's going to sell your house a lot quicker than the last guy?"

*Real estate (buyer):*
> "Do you feel like this could be the home for you and your family?"
> — "Yeah, we do."
> "Well hold on — why do you feel like it is though?"

*Life insurance (options-based variant):*
> "Which one of those would you lean more towards?"
> — "I think plan A."
> "Hold on — why do you feel like plan A compared to the others?"

## Key takeaways / CZ kontext

- **6-stage map** = mental scaffolding for any discovery call. Most CZ salespeople blend Stage 2 and 3 and skip 4 and 5 entirely. Adding 4 + 5 is where your calls level up.
- The **"100% love"** anchor works identically in Czech: "Jste 100% spokojený s tím, jak vám to funguje?" Nobody is.
- The **commitment probe** ("hold on, why do you feel like that?") triggers the real objection while the prospect is still on the call. This is the single highest-leverage CZ adaptation.
- **Tie consequence to what they said** — don't invent generic consequences.
