# Body

## The worst discovery questions

[0:33] These predictable questions trigger guarded surface answers:

- "What's your biggest challenge, Jim?"
- "What's your biggest concern about your XYZ thing?"
- "Can you tell me two problems you're having?"
- "Can you tell me some challenges keeping you awake at night?"
- "What's your biggest need?"
- "What's your biggest problem right now?"

Typical responses:
> "We're always looking for improvements — I wouldn't say we have challenges."
> "I mean, we do have this one problem, but it's not that bad."
> "I wouldn't say we have any needs right now — just looking at options."

## The Family Picture Frame analogy

[2:37]
> "Imagine your product or service feels the same as your competitors' to your prospects — whether we like it or not. You know some differences, but your competitors are also out there saying they're the best. So in your prospect's mind, it all looks the same."

> "Let's say you go to the store. Two identical family pictures. One you put in an immaculate frame — beautiful steel or wood. The other you put in a cracked frame with cobwebs, looks rundown. Which family picture are you going to choose? The one with the better frame. Same picture."

> "It doesn't matter what your solution is. The salesperson who wins is the one who masters how to frame that solution better in the prospect's mind and emotion."

## Reframe 1 — Life insurance with existing policy

[4:11] **Bad:** "What's your biggest challenge?"

**Good:** *(after situational discovery reveals they have $80k work policy)*

> "I mean, John — you guys already have this $80,000 work policy. *(skeptical/confused tone)* What's causing you to feel like that might not be enough for Cindy and the kids when you pass?"

[5:14]
> "Notice I'm kind of starting off skeptical, like 'you already have this — why am I even here?' And confused. Then ending in concern: like I'm concerned for you."

Mechanism: prospect now defends WHY their current setup isn't enough → they sell themselves.

## Reframe 2 — "100% satisfied" anchor

[6:23]
> "So, are you 100% satisfied with the results you've been getting?"

[6:57] **Industry plug-in:**
> "Are you 100% happy with the returns you've been getting?" (financial services)

> "Why 100%? Think about the person you're dating now. The first couple months they were perfect — angels on cloud 9. Six months later, you found something you don't like. Human beings don't like anything 100%."

[7:28]
> "Verbal pauses before AND after 100%. Forces the brain to focus on 100%. They'll say 'Well I wouldn't say 100%.' — 'Not 100%? What don't you like?' Now they tell you."

## Reframe 3 — Playful "doom and gloom"

[8:30]
> "I mean, you've been with XYZ company for six years. I mean, it can't be all doom and gloom over there. What have they done well for you so far?"

**Critical:** must use **playful tone**. Without playful tone, sounds weird/sarcastic.

> "Hold on, you guys have been with them for the past six years. I mean, it can't be all doom and gloom over there. What have they done well for you so far?"

When they answer: "Well, we really like X and Y."

**Quick snap probe** *(literally as they finish the sentence):*
> "Like — what would you change if you could though?"

## Key takeaways / CZ kontext

- **The Family Picture Frame analogy** translates perfectly to Czech and is one of the best mental models for pitch positioning. "Stejný produkt, jiný rámeček — výherce je ten, kdo umí to ten rámeček postavit."
- **Existing-vendor reframe** in CZ: *"Honzo, ty teď máš tu agenturu, co vám dělá web — co tě vede k pocitu, že to nemusí být úplně to nejlepší pro to, co potřebuješ?"*
- **"100% satisfied" anchor**: *"Jsi 100% spokojený s tím, kolik ti to teď přitahuje klientů?"* — even Czech reserved-types break here.
- **Playful "doom and gloom"** = *"No tak hele, nemůže to být všechno tak špatný — co se ti tam vlastně líbí?"* — releases tension.
- **Snap probe** = *"A co bys změnil, kdybys mohl, ale?"* — quickly after they list the positives.
