# Trial Lawyer Closing — Show Bruises + Diagnose Dismissiveness

## Context
Voss interviews trial lawyer Jefferson Fisher (later co-host on Voss's podcast). Two parts: (1) how to handle first-meeting credibility, (2) how to handle dismissive laughter from counterparts.

## Part 1: Show Your Bruises in Opening [00:00]

### The contrast in jury opening arguments
- Lawyer A: "Ladies and gentlemen, everything we've done is to the book, everything is perfect, zero complications."
- Lawyer B: "Facts will speak for themselves. We did some things we could have done better. Some things I don't like about our facts. But we can't change the facts. Life goes on. At the end of the day, find in our favor."
- **Lawyer B always wins.**

### Why showing bruises works [01:00]
- Lawyer A feels like he's selling you something. We know "everything is perfect" isn't true.
- Showing what you could do better + "coming from a place of wanting to help" = builds connection FASTER than trying to prove something.
- **"Connect rather than convince."**
- Be a straight shooter. "Tell the truth. We're not perfect. I'm not hiding from where we're not perfect."

### Punishment for inconsistency [02:00]
- "If you say something in an opening argument and it's not what the evidence shows at trial, they will punish you for it."

## Part 2: Dealing with Dismissive Laughter [03:01]

### Setup
- Question from Harper: "When you're talking to someone and it's a tactic — they laugh at you... How do you deal with that?"

### Voss's first reaction [03:55]
- Label it. "Sounds like I haven't earned your trust yet."
- Underlying message of the laugh: they don't trust you.

### Jefferson's diagnostic [04:25]
- Ask: how does the laugh make you feel?
- Laugh can be just as much an emotion-display as crying or shaking.
- Some people laugh when nervous — body's mechanism, not intentional dismissiveness.

### Direct check-in
- "Did this come across funny to you?"
- "Am I being taken seriously in this conversation?"
- "I need to know that I'm being taken seriously."

### When to walk [06:09]
- If it's dismissiveness, don't stay long.
- "The longer you stay in it, the more dismissive they are." Death spiral.
- "You're going to continue to feel like you need to prove yourself, like they need to apologize — and they'll feed on that."

### Voss's diagnostic tool: "Did You Mean?" [07:11]
- Two kinds of counterpart: (a) defense mechanism (heart not wrong, reaction inappropriate), (b) heart in wrong place.
- "Did you mean to be dismissive?" — said gently, not meanly.
- Their reaction tells you which one you have.
- If defensive → they catch themselves. Now productive convo possible.
- If wrong-hearted → walk away politely.
- "Delivery is everything."

## CZ adaptation notes
- Show bruises CZ for an opening: NOT "naše služba přinese každému klientovi {výsledek}." Instead: "Není to magic — u některých klientů to zafunguje líp, u některých hůř. Ale třeba {tvoje case study s čísly} — dám vám detaily." (case study ze sekce Důkazy v `knowledge/moje-nabidka.md`)
- Label dismissiveness CZ: "Vypadá to, že jsem si u vás ještě nezískala důvěru."
- "Did you mean to be dismissive?" CZ: **"Myslel jste to tak, jak to vyznělo?"** (klidným tónem, ne ostře).
- For sales calls when prospect makes jokes / brushes off Voss-style framework: diagnose via "myslel jsi to vážně?" → defensive responds gracefully, malicious doubles down → walk away.
